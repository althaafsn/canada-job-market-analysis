Yes. Let’s make this a **two-hour decision exercise** that you own. Its purpose is to choose a job direction worth testing. It does not need to become another portfolio project.

You have two useful facts already: you enjoy sustained problem solving, and your main goal is a software job. We should compare roles against both. A large market is useful only if you can realistically enter it.

One correction: we cannot reliably identify which role will be “best” next year. We can measure current opportunities, use published outlooks with care, and make a decision that we can revise.

I will help define the questions, review your choices, and diagnose problems. You will choose the metrics, collect the evidence, write the analysis, and explain the result. I will write code only when you request a specific part.

Here is a proposed question for the exercise:

> Which software job family offers me the best combination of accessible openings, compensation, and manageable preparation over the next three months?

Start with four families: frontend, backend/full-stack, data engineering, and AI engineering. These are proposed categories; job titles often overlap.

For metrics, I suggest:

| Metric | What you record | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Accessible openings | Unique postings you could realistically apply to, in the same search period and location | Measures your reachable market |
| Experience barrier | Required years and explicit seniority | Separates junior opportunities from total demand |
| Compensation | Posted salary range and currency; leave missing values empty | Allows a comparison without invented estimates |
| Preparation gap | Important requirements you cannot yet demonstrate | Makes the comparison specific to you |
| Work interest | Your 1–5 rating of the actual tasks | Helps you select work you can sustain |
| Evidence quality | Sample size, source, and collection date | Shows how much confidence the result deserves |

I would initially use annual compensation. Converting salary to hourly pay requires an assumption about working hours. Also, count advertised openings as a sample of demand; they are not a count of actual hires.

Keep the first analysis small:

| Time | Your task | Completion condition |
|---|---|---|
| 0–15 min | Set location, eligibility, role families, and metrics | One written research question and consistent inclusion rules |
| 15–30 min | Create a spreadsheet and classify two example postings | Columns are clear; you can explain each classification |
| 30–70 min | Collect up to five postings per family using the same search method | Each row has a source link; duplicates are removed |
| 70–95 min | Calculate simple summaries | Accessible share, experience requirements, and available salary figures |
| 95–110 min | Make one chart | Preparation gap on the horizontal axis; accessible share on the vertical axis; label each role and its sample size |
| 110–120 min | Write your interpretation | One direction to investigate, three limitations, and the next question |

Twenty postings can reveal useful differences, but they cannot establish the whole market. If you sample five postings per family, compare the **share that is accessible**, not the number collected. Keep salary separate if too few postings disclose it.

Do not make a combined “best job” score yet. Its weights could hide the tradeoffs we need to understand. First inspect the evidence; then decide how much you value pay, accessibility, and preparation time.

Afterward, we can choose one project whose tasks demonstrate requirements that appeared repeatedly in your chosen role family. That gives the project a clear reason to exist and a stopping point.
